[2026-04-16T16:09:00.5260000-05:00] the_biggest_rick: You need to keep in mind that you’re looking at a single asset here, not the entire portfolio. When you combine everything and equally weight all the ensembles, the Sharpe ratio should already improve. Matching the Sharpe of the underlying asset is actually the baseline goal—because what you’ve built is a system that effectively replicates the asset’s behavior while introducing entry and exit logic. That alone gives you a structured, algorithmic way to time the market, which is already an improvement in terms of control and flexibility.

The real advantage shows up when you expand to multiple assets. Once you introduce RSI-based allocation, capital shifts away from assets that are exiting and into those with stronger signals. That’s where the Sharpe improves further—because you’re now benefiting from cross-sectional momentum. In other words, you’re allocating toward assets that are outperforming relative to the rest. That’s exactly what the gating mechanism is designed to do. It’s also why focusing on a single ensemble misses the point entirely.

As for performance thresholds, Sharpe ratios above 1 are the minimum you’re aiming for. That indicates at least some level of generalization over a period like 3.5 years, which already includes multiple market regimes and a few corrections. If the system maintains that level, it suggests your timing logic is still outperforming a simple buy-and-hold approach. Adding the gating layer on top completes the full strategy.
----------------------------------------
[2026-04-16T16:09:11.4820000-05:00] the_biggest_rick: So don’t get discouraged if results fluctuate—that’s expected. Financial data is non-stationary, meaning it constantly evolves. You can’t apply something like a strict 5% overfitting rule the same way you would in traditional machine learning, where data is assumed to be stationary. Here, the real test is whether the model continues to meet that baseline Sharpe threshold while the environment changes. That’s what demonstrates robustness. Parameter sensitivity analysis also plays a role in confirming this.

If you want a simple way to validate the idea, just equally weight all the ensembles without applying RSI rotation. You should already see an improvement in the Sharpe ratio. If you don’t, then something else is likely wrong. From there, adding the rotation and gating should enhance performance even further.
----------------------------------------
[2026-04-16T16:10:05.3140000-05:00] the_biggest_rick: You also have to recognize that the rolling Sharpe ratio naturally fluctuates and can decline over time. That’s exactly where RSI rotation becomes useful. It helps you enter positions when returns are expanding significantly relative to average losses—essentially when momentum is strong. In practice, that often means you’re entering an asset during periods when its rolling Sharpe is elevated.

Because of that, you’re not fully exposed to all the weaker periods of a single asset. Instead, you can sidestep many of those drawdowns by rotating into other assets that are uncorrelated and currently showing stronger behavior. That rotation effect is key—it allows you to continuously reallocate capital toward areas where risk-adjusted returns are improving.

A good example of this kind of behavior can be seen in sectors like energy at certain times, where relative strength stands out compared to the broader market. In those situations, rotation naturally pulls you into those outperforming segments while avoiding weaker ones.

[2026-04-30T13:25:31.8200000-05:00] thijs_71567: During the inner loop, for each parameter combination, the strategy is trained on the training split and then evaluated on both the training data and the validation data. This produces an in-sample Sharpe ratio and a validation Sharpe ratio. I then compute a combined score defined as the validation Sharpe minus the absolute difference between the training and validation Sharpe. This means that parameter sets are rewarded not only for achieving strong out-of-sample performance within the cross-validation, but also for showing consistency between training and validation results, which helps reduce overfitting.
The parameter set with the highest combined score is selected within the inner loop and then applied to the outer test fold. This outer fold acts as a true out-of-sample evaluation for that iteration. The same process is repeated across all outer folds so that each segment of the data is used once as an out-of-sample test. (Until here the whole process is just to evaluate the parameter selection process)
After completing the nested cross-validation, I then perform one final inner cross-validation over the entire training dataset, excluding the holdout period, using the same scoring method. This step produces a final set of parameters based on all available training data. Those parameters are subsequently applied to the holdout period, which serves as the final and completely unseen out-of-sample test.

[2026-07-25T08:16:45.7200000-05:00] armored3551: Hey! Im a little late but I do have some advice as I've tried a similar thing before. Biggest thing is that your noise ceiling is enormous.

Best-of-N under a pure noise null scales as √(2·ln N). At 14M combos that's ~5.7 t-units, so your best result needs to clear that before it means anything. Most indicator grids top out around 2-3. Worth computing that number and drawing it as a line on your charts. (If your not sure how to do the calculations or what they mean claude can run them just lookover it thoroughly)  also those red sensitivity charts aren't really a thing to fix later, they're the biggest red flag. If IS is already unstable, OOS can't rescue it. 

The fix for exactly your situation is Deflated Sharpe. It takes your number of trials, the variance across those trials, and your returns' skew and tells you the Sharpe you'd expect from the luckiest of 14M random tries. Your observed Sharpe has to beat that, not zero. DSR > 0.95 is the usual bar. It's maybe 30 lines of numpy and it'll re-rank your whole Excel sheet in one pass. My guess is most of the ensemble stuff drops out, since combining indicators multiplies trials fast.

Happy to expand more, I've been running a similar meat grinder and mostly producing extremely well documented negatives lol
