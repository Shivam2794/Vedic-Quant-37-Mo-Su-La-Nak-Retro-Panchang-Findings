[2025-10-30T06:31:19.0370000-05:00] the_biggest_rick: *Story: Econometrics vs. Backtesting — The Two Sciences of the Market*

There’s this divide in how people try to “understand” the market.
One side comes from econometrics, the other from the algorithmic and ML world.

Econometrics is like the classic academic science of markets. It builds models from theory, with equations full of betas, fixed effects, and “ceteris paribus” assumptions. The idea is to isolate one variable’s effect while holding everything else constant. You take accounting data, macro indicators, valuation ratios, and run regressions to see what should influence returns. If the math checks out, you get a statistically significant relationship — maybe something like “cash-flow yield predicts excess return.” But the truth is, these models live in a controlled, theoretical bubble. They describe what might drive performance, not necessarily what will.

Now, compare that to the algorithmic trader or ML engineer.
They don’t start with assumptions, they start with data. They test thousands of combinations — lookback windows, thresholds, parameter grids — and find what actually works. It’s not about why it works, it’s about if it works.
You run a grid search on a moving average crossover, RSI, or volatility breakout system. You optimize lookbacks from 5 to 200 days, test them over ten years of data, and check the Sharpe ratios, drawdowns, and hit rates. Every run gives you a measurable outcome. You get validation curves, not theoretical betas. When you finish, you have an actual entry and exit rule that’s tuned, that you can backtest in-sample, stress test out-of-sample, and deploy.

[2025-10-30T06:31:43.2410000-05:00] the_biggest_rick: Machine learning goes even deeper.
It takes historical data, learns from patterns that consistently led to certain outcomes, and updates its internal structure to predict those outcomes better. It’s supervised by results — the label tells it whether it guessed right or wrong. The more data, the sharper it gets. You can visualize how it learned, see which features matter, and then benchmark it live. That’s science with feedback.

Econometrics, in contrast, works more like a simulation built on beliefs about how the economy functions. It uses lagged variables to mimic causality and claims to “control for” other effects. But the feedback is weaker. You don’t retrain; you re-estimate and test significance again. There’s rarely a validation dataset, just robustness checks that depend on the same theory that built the model in the first place.

In trading, this difference matters a lot.
A regression might tell you that “low price-to-book firms outperform,” but that’s descriptive. It’s a statement about a relationship, not a trading rule. A backtest, on the other hand, tells you how to use that information — when to enter, when to exit, what lookback gives stability, what volatility filter avoids traps. It turns relationships into mechanics. You can visualize the result as an equity curve, not a t-stat.

That’s why many econometric findings don’t survive contact with the market.
They aren’t wrong, they’re just not operational. They explain a tendency under ideal assumptions, while algorithmic backtesting shows behavior under real conditions — slippage, regime shifts, noise, and all. One is theory-first, the other is data-first. One seeks meaning, the other seeks function.

The premise of trading as a trend follower is exactly what you think it is — trading in the direction of the trend. As seen in the above image, one way to trend trade is to look at an asset with a resistance line. Once the price breaks through resistance, a trend trader would trade in the direction of the breakout [1]. In our above example, the trader would take a long position (betting the price will go up for profit). After a period of time, the trend normally stops for some reason, and the price of the asset will reverse. Once the reversal occurs, a trend trader would close the position for a profit or enter a short position, following the trend again.

From my own personal research, trend following tends to have good payouts (i.e. you gain much more than risk such as $2 reward to $1 risk or better). The downside of this strategy is trends do not happen too often, so you should expect to have a lot of your trades lose before you get the big trend trade. Of course, you can make alterations to this basic trend following strategy to improve other aspects, but I am simply explaining what would happen if you took up this strategy as a beginner. Another thing to note is trend follow can be psychologically difficult to execute. It takes a certain personality type to have conviction to take a bunch of losing little trades before getting the big trade. Once the big trade occurs, the trend trader also needs to be careful not to take profits too early in order to be profitable from their smaller losing trades.

On the other hand, mean reversion trading makes an assumption that prices converge towards a mean. Once the price gets too far away from the mean, a mean reversion trader thinks the price will return towards this mean [2]. [2025-12-27T10:58:41.8360000-06:00] the_biggest_rick: 2.3.2. Boruta feature selection
Boruta is a feature selection algorithm built upon a Random Forest
classification (Kursa & Rudnicki, 2010). Boruta tests individual features
iteratively and eliminates them from the selected feature list if they are
less relevant compared to random probes. (Kursa & Rudnicki, 2010)
described the Boruta algorithm in following steps:

1. Add “shadow attributes” to the dataset that are copies of existing
attributes.
2. Shuffle the shadow attributes to ensure they are not correlated to the
target. These attributes act as the random probes referred earlier.
3. Random Forest classification is performed on this dataset which has
both original and shadow attributes. Then z-score is computed for
the attributes.
4. Maximum z score is computed for shadow attributes and termed as
MZSA. Each feature’s z score is compared against MZSA. Features
with higher z score are labeled important and features with lower z
score are labeled unimportant.
5. Each feature’s importance is evaluated using two-sided MZSA test.
For features with significantly less MZSA are eliminated from
selected feature set. Features with significantly high MZSA are
retained in selected feature set.
6. All shadow attributes are dropped and the process is repeated again
until all features in feature set are labeled important or unimportant
or a predefined stop criteria is achieved.
