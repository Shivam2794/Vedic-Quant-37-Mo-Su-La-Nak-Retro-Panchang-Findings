[2025-10-18T04:58:02.3840000-05:00] the_biggest_rick: *regarding if optuna is an option for gridsearching*

Optuna is definitely a great option. The main thing to remember, though, is that Optuna uses Bayesian optimization.
In terms of computation, it’s much faster — but unlike a grid search, which brute-forces through every possible combination, Bayesian optimization relies on a probabilistic search. It assumes a relatively well-behaved landscape, meaning it can often find a good combination more efficiently.
However, that also means it might skip over a potential best combination, especially if the search space is rugged.
That’s why, if computation isn’t an issue, I tend to prefer a brute-force grid search. It’s built into VectorBT, requires less coding, and is generally an easier, lower-error option — especially if you don’t have a heavy software engineering background.

Probabilistic acceptance: The algorithm can accept worse solutions, especially at high "temperatures," to explore the solution space. Sensitivity to parameters: The final outcome is highly dependent on the "cooling schedule" and other parameters, which are problem-specific. This upward bias comes from several economic and behavioral factors — inflation, rising costs, long-term economic growth, and general investor optimism. [2025-11-15T11:28:14.5600000-06:00] the_biggest_rick: The way I look at it is this: the sensitivity analysis is what tells you whether you’re overfitting. The test data (OS) is only there to show that the strategy actually survives real conditions.

[2026-01-26T07:37:21.1270000-06:00] the_biggest_rick: ABOUT POLYMARKETS

The exact turn point or power of certain static signals, like technical indicators, comes from what actually carries predictive power. For example, in general, a price can mean revert at a certain Z-score of a given value. In general, the strongest asset with the most momentum has the strongest autocorrelated pattern, and this is the most likely to play out.

The most important thing about sensitivity analysis is that you’re comparing five different tests across five different parameter combinations — maybe after something like a Boruta pass — and then selecting the one with the tightest range. Smaller is better.

The biggest deviations usually show up in the trade count. If trade count suddenly starts deviating by more than, say, five trades, you immediately know something is wrong with the timing at least. That’s a red flag.

Sharpe is an aggregate — it’s a composition of multiple trades and multiple metrics stacked on top of each other. Because of that, it’s much more likely to deviate even if just two small inputs move slightly. So it’s naturally going to look different. The Sharpe ratio isn’t really the best standalone metric to rely on here.

But if, during sensitivity analysis, the number of trades starts deviating heavily, that’s a clear sign you’re overfitting to a specific curve pattern, which is obviously not favorable.

The whole point is to make sure there’s a range — a safety buffer — and that performance isn’t overly sensitive to one single parameter combination.

[2026-05-17T13:30:07.3060000-05:00] the_biggest_rick: A leveraged ETF like TQQQ resets every day. That daily reset means if the market chops sideways — up 3%, down 3%, up 3%, down 3% — the LETF bleeds money even though the underlying went nowhere. That's volatility decay. The bumpier the ride, the more it bleeds, regardless of direction.

GARCH is a model that tries to predict how bumpy tomorrow will be based on how bumpy recent days were. That part is fine.
The problem is what GARCH does with that bumpiness number. It assumes "bumpy" only tells you about risk — how wide the range of outcomes is. It does not know that on an LETF, bumpy also mechanically eats your return. The bumpiness is both a risk signal and a return signal at the same time, and GARCH can only see it as one thing.

So when you feed GARCH's vol estimate into a position-sizing rule, you're getting half the picture. The model says "vol is high, size down for safety" — but it's missing "vol is high, and by the way you're also losing money to decay even if you're right about direction." You think you're managing risk; you're actually ignoring a return drag that scales with the square of the volatility you just measured.

The fix is what you'd expect: model the volatility on the underlying (QQQ), then separately subtract the known decay penalty for the LETF. Two clean numbers instead of one muddy one.
